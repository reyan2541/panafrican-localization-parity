\begin{abstract}
Pedestrian detection is a safety-critical component of autonomous driving and
intelligent surveillance, where equitable performance across demographic groups
is essential for trustworthy deployment. Fairness research in this domain has
evaluated performance almost exclusively through detection recall, that is,
whether a person is found at all, leaving the precision of the predicted
bounding box unexamined. Because existing audits reduce localization to a binary
hit-or-miss threshold, systematic disparities in how tightly detectors localize
different groups remain invisible, even though loose boxes directly compromise
downstream distance and trajectory estimates. This paper introduces
\emph{Localization Parity}, a fairness criterion that requires equal expected
Intersection over Union across demographic groups conditional on detection. We
present the first confounder-controlled audit spanning four driving datasets
(BDD100K, CityPersons, EuroCity-Day, and EuroCity-Night), three detector
families (YOLOX, RetinaNet, and Cascade~R-CNN), and two demographic axes
(gender and age), together with a demographic-weighted bounding box regression
intervention. Female and child pedestrians receive significantly looser boxes in
all twelve dataset and detector combinations, with mean gaps of $1.40$ and
$3.27$ percentage points. These gaps survive scale stratification in $35$ of
$36$ and $24$ of $30$ cells, respectively. The proposed intervention closes the
training-set gap by $46.8\%$, yet fails to generalize to validation data.
Localization Parity therefore exposes a previously unmeasured and
safety-relevant axis of algorithmic unfairness, and the resulting fairness
generalization gap poses a concrete open problem for equitable perception
systems.
\keywords{Localization Parity \and Algorithmic Fairness \and Pedestrian Detection
\and Bounding Box Regression \and Demographic Bias \and Object Detection}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Pedestrian detection is central to modern computer vision pipelines for
self-driving cars, surveillance, and traffic infrastructure. The cost of error
is not shared evenly across demographic groups. Prior work has shown that
\emph{detection recall} is unequal: Wilson et~al.~\cite{wilson2019predictive}
report lower average precision for darker-skinned pedestrians, while
Brandao~\cite{brandao2019age} and Li et~al.~\cite{li2025bias} find substantially
higher miss rates for children than for adults across many detectors.

What these audits leave unmeasured is \emph{localization quality}. Once a
detector draws a box, the tightness of that box shapes every estimate that
follows: position, distance to the ego vehicle, predicted trajectory, and
braking urgency. Two detections can both pass the conventional
IoU\,$\geq$\,$0.5$ hit threshold and still differ sharply in geometry. A box at
IoU~$0.51$ and a box at IoU~$0.91$ look identical under recall-based fairness
metrics, yet they feed very different signals into downstream modules. If one
demographic group systematically receives looser boxes, that group
systematically receives worse downstream estimates, and no current fairness
benchmark would notice.

We formalize this overlooked criterion as \textbf{Localization Parity (LP)}:
equality of expected Intersection over Union (IoU) across demographic groups,
conditional on successful detection and after controlling for confounders such as
object scale. We then run the first multi-dataset, multi-detector LP audit and
test a training-time mitigation.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A formal definition of Localization Parity as a group fairness criterion
      on box regression quality, orthogonal to detection parity, with a clear
      link to existing fairness notions for classification and regression
      (Section~\ref{sec:lp}).
\item The first confounder-controlled LP audit across four autonomous driving
      datasets, three detector families spanning single-stage and two-stage
      designs, and two demographic axes: \emph{gender} (female vs.\ male) and
      \emph{age} (child vs.\ adult). Race and skin tone are not audited in this
      study (Section~\ref{sec:audit}).
\item Empirical evidence that female and child pedestrians receive consistently
      lower IoU boxes (mean gaps $-1.40$\,pp and $-3.27$\,pp), surviving
      ICON$^2$ scale stratification~\cite{sudhakar2023icon2}.
\item Demographic-Weighted Bounding Box Regression (DWBBR), which closes the
      training gap by $46.8\%$ yet fails to transfer cleanly to held-out data. We
      document this \emph{fairness generalization gap} as an open problem.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Fairness in computer vision.}
Group fairness (demographic parity and equalized odds)~\cite{hardt2016equality},
individual fairness, and calibration form the classical toolkit. Vision audits
usually operationalize group fairness through equal detection rates or equal
average precision. Wilson et~al.~\cite{wilson2019predictive} established
predictive inequity across Fitzpatrick skin-tone groups on BDD100K using AP.
Schumann et~al.~\cite{schumann2021step} critique binary third-party gender
labels. Fair regression definitions~\cite{agarwal2019fair} constrain disparities
in continuous prediction error; LP applies the same idea to the IoU of matched
detections (Section~\ref{sec:lp}).

\paragraph{Pedestrian detection and demographic bias.}
BDD100K~\cite{yu2020bdd100k}, CityPersons~\cite{zhang2017citypersons}, and
EuroCity Persons~\cite{braun2019eurocity} are the dominant driving benchmarks.
Li et~al.~\cite{li2025bias} and Brandao~\cite{brandao2019age} show elevated
child miss rates across many detectors. Khoshkdahan et~al.~\cite{khoshkdahan2025pose}
study pose and occlusion as structural confounders of detection accuracy. All of
these works evaluate hit-or-miss outcomes rather than continuous localization
quality.

\paragraph{Confounder control and fair training.}
ICON$^2$~\cite{sudhakar2023icon2} stratifies by scale and occlusion so that
demographic gaps cannot be attributed to size alone; we apply it to IoU rather
than AP. Loss reweighting and Group DRO~\cite{sagawa2020dro} are standard
tools for fair training, but they have been used mainly for classification or
recall rather than for the bounding box regression branch. Focal
Loss~\cite{lin2017focal} reweights hard classification examples; DWBBR instead
reweights the CIoU regression term by demographic group performance.

\section{Localization Parity}
\label{sec:lp}

\subsection{Formal Definition}
\label{sec:lp-def}

Let $\mathcal{D}=\{(b_i,\hat{g}_i,g_i)\}$ be the set of successfully detected
instances, where $b_i$ is the predicted box, $\hat{g}_i$ the ground-truth box,
and $g_i\in\{g_1,\ldots,g_k\}$ the demographic group of instance~$i$. Matching
follows the COCO convention: a prediction counts as a detection only if
$\mathrm{IoU}(b_i,\hat{g}_i)\geq 0.5$. Localization Parity requires
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{IoU}(b_i,\hat{g}_i)\mid g_i=g_a\right]
\;=\;
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathrm{IoU}(b_i,\hat{g}_i)\mid g_i=g_b\right]
\label{eq:lp}
\end{equation}
for every pair of groups $a\neq b$, after controlling for instance scale. We
report the signed gap
\[
\Delta\mathrm{IoU}
=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{IoU}\mid g_{\mathrm{priv}}]
-\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{IoU}\mid g_{\mathrm{dis}}],
\]
where $g_{\mathrm{priv}}$ denotes the privileged group (male, adult) and
$g_{\mathrm{dis}}$ the disadvantaged group (female, child). A negative gap means
the disadvantaged group receives worse boxes.

LP is deliberately conditional on detection. It equalizes box quality among
people who were found, and is therefore orthogonal to detection parity (equal
recall or miss rate). A detector can satisfy one while violating the other; both
are needed for safety-relevant fairness.

\subsection{Relation to Existing Fairness Criteria}
\label{sec:lp-theory}

LP sits at the intersection of group fairness and fair regression:
\begin{itemize}
\item Relative to demographic parity and equalized odds for
      classification~\cite{hardt2016equality,calders2010discrimination}, LP
      replaces a binary outcome (detected or not) with a continuous quality
      score (IoU), evaluated only on the detected set. This is analogous to
      equalized opportunity applied to localization quality.
\item Relative to fair regression~\cite{agarwal2019fair}, which seeks bounded
      disparity in expected loss across groups, LP uses the loss
      $\ell=1-\mathrm{IoU}$ on matched boxes.
\item Relative to AP-based audits~\cite{wilson2019predictive}, LP isolates the
      localization component that AP mixes with classification confidence and
      ranking.
\end{itemize}
LP does not claim individual fairness or calibration. It is a group-level
criterion because the safety harm of interest, systematically worse downstream
estimates for a demographic group, is itself group structured.

\subsection{Why Small IoU Gaps Matter Downstream}
\label{sec:safety}

A natural question is whether gaps of $1$--$3$\,pp IoU are practically
consequential. Under a pinhole projection, relative depth error scales with
relative box-height error. For a pedestrian at $30$\,m whose true box height is
$80$\,px, a $2$\,pp mean IoU deficit (roughly $2$--$4$\,px of systematic height
or vertical-center error) translates into about $0.7$--$1.5$\,m of depth bias.
That is enough to alter time-to-collision estimates at urban speeds and to shift
predicted crossing trajectories. Because trajectory and collision modules are
typically nonlinear in box geometry, group-level mean gaps of this magnitude can
compound across the planning stack even when every detection still clears the
$0.5$ IoU threshold. We therefore treat LP gaps in this range as
safety-relevant, not merely statistically detectable. A full end-to-end
propagation study remains future work (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}

We evaluate on four pedestrian benchmarks chosen for geographic, sensor, and
lighting diversity:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{BDD100K}~\cite{yu2020bdd100k}: large-scale US dashcam data with
      about $100{,}000$ annotated pedestrian boxes and binary male/female
      labels.
\item \textbf{CityPersons}~\cite{zhang2017citypersons}: about $19{,}000$ boxes
      over $2{,}975$ Cityscapes street-level images, with careful occlusion
      annotation.
\item \textbf{EuroCity-Day} and \textbf{EuroCity-Night}~\cite{braun2019eurocity}:
      subsets of EuroCity Persons (about $238{,}200$ boxes over $47{,}300$
      images from $31$ European cities), split by lighting.
\end{itemize}
Demographic labels for CityPersons and EuroCity use augmented annotations where
available. Detections with missing or ambiguous labels are dropped. Label
quality limitations are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:ethics}.

\subsection{Detectors}
\label{sec:detectors}

We cover the three main detector design families with public pretrained weights
and no fine-tuning in the audit phase:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{YOLOX}~\cite{ge2021yolox}: single-stage, anchor-free;
\item \textbf{RetinaNet}~\cite{lin2017focal}: single-stage, anchor-based;
\item \textbf{Cascade R-CNN}~\cite{cai2021cascade}: two-stage, with cascaded IoU
      refinement aimed at tighter localization.
\end{itemize}
The mitigation experiments use \textbf{YOLOv8n} (Ultralytics), the lightweight
YOLO variant that serves as our trainable single-stage backbone.

\subsection{Demographic Axes}
\label{sec:axes}

The two protected attributes studied are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Gender:} female vs.\ male;
\item \textbf{Age:} child vs.\ adult.
\end{enumerate}
Race and skin tone are not among the audited axes. An earlier pilot examined
skin tone, but the labeled sample was too small and the categories too coarse
for reliable statistical claims. We therefore exclude it here and mark it as
future work (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}).

\subsection{Audit Protocol and Confounder Control}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Matching.}
Predictions are matched to ground truth by greedy, confidence-sorted IoU
assignment. Matches with IoU\,$\geq$\,$0.5$ enter the LP analysis; false
positives are excluded.

\paragraph{Confounders.}
The primary confounder is \textbf{object scale} (box height), because female and
child pedestrians tend to occupy smaller image regions, and small objects are
harder to localize for any reason. Where annotations permit, we also consider
\textbf{occlusion}. Following ICON$^2$~\cite{sudhakar2023icon2}, we bin
ground-truth instances within each dataset into short ($\leq$33rd height
percentile), medium (34th--66th), and tall ($\geq$67th) strata, then recompute
per-group mean IoU inside each bin. A gap that remains significant within strata
cannot be attributed to scale alone.

\paragraph{Statistical testing.}
Within each cell and each stratum we compare group IoU distributions with the
two-sided Mann--Whitney $U$ test. IoU distributions are non-Gaussian and
heavy-tailed, so a non-parametric rank test is appropriate. Significance is
declared at $p<0.05$. We report both raw and stratified gaps.

\section{Demographic-Weighted Bounding Box Regression}
\label{sec:dwbbr}

DWBBR applies the Group DRO reweighting principle~\cite{sagawa2020dro} to the
CIoU regression branch. Standard YOLO training minimizes
$L_{\mathrm{class}}+\lambda L_{\mathrm{reg}}$. DWBBR replaces the regression
term with a group-weighted sum:
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{DWBBR}}
= L_{\mathrm{class}}
+ \lambda\sum_i w_{g_i}\,\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{CIoU}}(b_i,\hat{g}_i),
\label{eq:dwbbr}
\end{equation}
where $w_g \propto 1/\mathbb{E}_{\mathrm{train}}[\mathrm{IoU}\mid g]$ and
weights are renormalized each epoch so that $\sum_g w_g=1$. No architecture
change is required. We override the Ultralytics training callback to recompute
weights from per-group training CIoU and scale the regression loss before
backpropagation.

\paragraph{Training protocol.}
Both DWBBR and an identically configured baseline (unweighted YOLOv8n) are
trained on a $1{,}693$-image BDD100K subset with reliable gender labels
($64.8\%$ male and $35.2\%$ female instances), for $50$ epochs, batch size~$8$,
image size $416\times416$, $\mathrm{lr}_0=0.01$ with cosine decay, on a single
NVIDIA RTX~3050. Validation uses a disjoint $423$-image subset. We replicate
with seeds $\{42,123,7\}$.

\subsection{Phase 2 and Phase 3: DWBBR Intervention}
\label{sec:intervention}

\paragraph{Training dynamics.}
On the training set, DWBBR behaves as intended
(Figure~\ref{fig:training}): the female to male CIoU gap narrows from
$-2.82$\,pp at epoch~$1$ to $-1.50$\,pp at epoch~$50$ ($46.8\%$ relative
reduction), while the female loss weight drifts toward parity as the per-group
IoU ratio improves.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{figures/fig3_training.png}
\caption{DWBBR training dynamics: LP gap convergence over $50$ epochs on
BDD100K.}
\label{fig:training}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Validation.}
On held-out data the picture changes (Table~\ref{tab:val},
Figure~\ref{fig:val}). Under the primary seed, DWBBR female CIoU is $0.7715$
against baseline $0.7790$; the female to male gap widens from $-1.52$\,pp
(baseline) to $-2.74$\,pp (DWBBR). A one-sided Mann--Whitney test finds no
significant female improvement ($p=0.71$). Across three seeds the mean
validation gap moves from $-1.78\pm0.48$\,pp (baseline) to
$-1.36\pm0.63$\,pp (DWBBR), a $23.6\%$ mean reduction accompanied by higher
variance and unstable best-checkpoint epochs ($\{15,17,21\}$ for DWBBR versus
$\{34,17,24\}$ for baseline).

\paragraph{Fairness generalization gap.}
DWBBR reliably closes the training LP gap, yet the improvement does not transfer
cleanly to validation: variance rises, checkpoint selection becomes unstable,
and under the primary seed the validation gap worsens. This aligns with known
limits of group reweighting under train and test demographic
shift~\cite{sagawa2020dro}. We report it as a finding rather than as a claim
that all mitigations must fail, and we treat fairness-aware checkpoint selection
and stronger DRO-style objectives as concrete next steps.

\section{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}

Demographic labels in BDD100K are third-party visual inferences: binary gender,
no self-identification, and undocumented annotation guidelines. These raise
consent, accuracy, and inclusivity concerns~\cite{schumann2021step}. LP is a
proxy for box-quality fairness, not a guarantee of equal downstream risk.
Nonlinear collision estimators can map equal mean IoU to unequal outcomes.
Fairer localization can reduce collision risk and can also improve
re-identification and surveillance. We publish because we judge the near-term
safety benefit to outweigh that dual-use risk, while acknowledging that the
judgment is contestable.

The study examines only gender and age. We do not report race or skin-tone LP
results here. Intersectional analyses (for example, young female pedestrians)
and broader attributes such as disability and body type remain open. Binary
gender labels also exclude non-binary identities from the analysis.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Localization Parity is a previously unmeasured, safety-relevant fairness
criterion for pedestrian detection. Across four datasets and three detector
families, female and child pedestrians receive systematically lower IoU boxes,
and the gaps survive scale stratification. Demographic-weighted regression
closes the training gap by $46.8\%$ but does not generalize reliably. That
result establishes the fairness generalization gap as an open research problem.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The intervention is evaluated on BDD100K with YOLOv8n only. The validation child
cohort is small ($n=17/16$). Demographic labels are third-party and binary. We
do not yet quantify end-to-end propagation of IoU gaps into planning errors.

\paragraph{Future work.}
Promising next steps include cross-dataset and cross-architecture DWBBR
evaluation, fairness-aware checkpoint selection, Group DRO and oversampling
baselines, intersectional and skin-tone analyses, and closed-loop simulation of
how $1$--$3$\,pp IoU disparities propagate into distance, time-to-collision, and
braking decisions, including domains beyond autonomous driving such as
surveillance and robotics.
